Almost every classic result in time-series econometrics is proved under an assumption that is
rarely stated out loud: the process is stationary. Regressions still run without it. They simply
stop meaning what you think they mean.

## What stationary means, in one paragraph

A series is (weakly) stationary when its mean, variance and autocovariance do not depend on the
calendar. The mean can be non-zero, the level can drift around a trend, but the *rules generating
the series* must be the same in 2011 and in 2026. If they are not, a sample mean is not an
estimate of anything: it is an average of different processes, and a t-statistic on it has no
fixed distribution to compare against.

## The unit root

The distinction that matters in practice is between two ways of writing "the level wanders":

Trend-stationary, where shocks decay:

$$
y_t = a + b t + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t \sim \text{white noise}
$$

And difference-stationary, where shocks accumulate forever:

$$
y_t = y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t
$$

Both look like a rising line on a chart. They are not the same object. In the first, a shock of
size $\varepsilon$ has an effect that dies out; the forecast eventually returns to the trend. In
the second, the effect of that shock is permanent: the entire future path is shifted by
$\varepsilon$, and the variance of $y_t$ grows without bound, $\operatorname{Var}(y_t) = t \sigma^2$.

Financial levels - prices, indices, exchange rates - are almost always the second kind. Returns
are almost always the first. That is why the default in empirical work is to model differences,
and why the argument over "level or difference" is really an argument about which process
generated the data.

## Spurious regression

The failure mode has a name and a demonstration. Take two independent random walks:

```python
import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
x = np.cumsum(rng.normal(size=250))
y = np.cumsum(rng.normal(size=250))   # independent of x by construction

model = sm.OLS(y, sm.add_constant(x)).fit()
print(f"t = {model.tvalues[1]:.2f}   r2 = {model.rsquared:.2f}")
```

Run it and you will get t-statistics well past the conventional critical values and an
$R^2$ that looks respectable. The two series share nothing. The regression is picking up the
fact that both wandered, not a relationship between them. This is Granger and Newbold's
spurious regression, and the only defence is to test the order of integration first.

## Two tests, and what they disagree about

**Augmented Dickey-Fuller.** Regress $\Delta y_t$ on $y_{t-1}$ and lagged differences, and
test whether the coefficient on the level is zero. The null hypothesis is a unit root, so a small
p-value is evidence *against* non-stationarity - a double negative that trips up most first
readings. Lags are included to absorb autocorrelation, and the lag length changes the answer more
often than people admit.

**KPSS.** Same question, inverted null: the series is stationary as the null. Run both. When ADF
fails to reject and KPSS rejects, the case for differencing is strong. When ADF rejects and KPSS
does not, you have a stationary series and no reason to difference it. When they disagree with
each other, that is usually the signature of a near-unit-root process or a structural break, and
the honest response is to look at the plot rather than to pick the test you prefer.

## What to do about it

- Test before you model, and report the lag length and the deterministic terms you chose.
- Difference when the level has a unit root, and remember that differencing removes the
  long-run component along with the trend.
- If two $I(1)$ series are cointegrated, do not difference them away: the cointegrating
  regression is the result, and the error-correction term is the interesting part.
- A structural break looks like a unit root to both tests. If there is a known regime change in
  the sample, model it explicitly instead of differencing around it.

Stationarity is not a technicality to get past. It decides whether the number you report is a
feature of the data or an artefact of the sample window.
