An inversion is a shape on a chart, and shapes are cheap. The part that carries
information is not that a long rate fell below a short one, but how long the two
stay that way, how deep the gap gets, and what else is moving while they do. Read
on its own, the sign of the spread is one bit. Read as a run, it is a description
of a state - and states persist in a way that single readings do not.

## The spread is one subtraction

The term spread is the difference between a long rate and a short rate:

$$
\text{spread}_t = y^{(10)}_t - y^{(2)}_t
$$

That is the whole construction. Everything interesting lives in how the resulting
series is summarised. A level tells you where the curve sits today; a sign tells
you which way it slopes; a duration tells you how long it has sloped that way, and
the duration is the one most often left out of the summary.

## A state, not an event

An inversion is described as *happening*, which makes it sound like a moment. In
the illustrative sample the spread crosses zero and then stays on the same side of
it for a stretch measured in months, drifting deeper and shallower within that run
without ever resurfacing. The distinction matters because a run has a beginning, a
depth and an end, and the run itself is the object the historical record is about.

Depth behaves differently from duration. In the same sample, the deepest reading of
a run is not a good guide to how long the run lasts: the two move together loosely
at best. If a summary reports only the deepest point, it is reporting the noisiest
number in the episode and discarding the most stable one.

## Why the lag is the hard part

The notable feature of an inverted curve is that it is historically associated with
a slowdown that arrives later - often considerably later. That is an association
between a state and a subsequent state, measured across a handful of episodes of a
slow variable. It is not a clock. A spread that turns negative while nothing else
moves is not a confirmed early warning; it is one variable moving, and the record
says only that this particular variable has historically moved ahead of a different
one.

Two mistakes follow from confusing the lag with a schedule. The first is treating
an inversion as an event to be dated precisely, when the run has no single moment
that can carry that weight. The second is reading the depth of the current run as
the size of what follows, which the illustrative sample does not support and which
the construction of the variable never implied.

## What a careful reading looks like

Three steps, none of them predictive. First, describe the run rather than the
crossing: its length, its depth and its direction of travel. Second, compare it
with the other slow variables you already track, because an inversion alongside
broad stability is a different description from an inversion alongside several
other variables moving. Third, hold the sample size in view - a pattern supported
by a few dozen observations of a monthly variable is a description, and
descriptions are what the record actually provides.

## What this is not

Nothing here is a forecast, a probability, or a rule for acting. The illustrative
figures come from a single synthetic path and are not evidence that any
relationship holds in general. The useful claim is narrow: an inversion is a state
with a length, the historical association is a lag rather than a trigger, and a
summary that reports only the crossing is reporting the smallest part of the
observation.
