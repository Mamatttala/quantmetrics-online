If a portfolio note says two exposures diversify each other, it is really quoting
one number: a correlation, measured over some window, at some point in the past.
That is a sample estimate. It has a standard error, it drifts, and under stress
it has a documented tendency to move in an unhelpful direction.

## The check

Pick the pairs the argument actually depends on - usually two or three, not
thirty. For each, compute a rolling correlation at a short window and at a long
one, and plot the two together. Then look at the difference rather than at either
line:

$$
\Delta_t = \rho^{\text{short}}_t - \rho^{\text{long}}_t
$$

Two patterns are worth naming. A sustained positive `\Delta_t` means the pair
has recently been moving together more than its history suggests. A sustained
negative `\Delta_t` means the opposite. Neither is a forecast; both are a
statement about whether the assumption you are relying on is currently supported
by the data in front of you.

## Why the difference and not the level

The level is the number everyone already quotes, and it is the noisiest one. In
the illustrative sample, a short-window estimate on a pair with a long-run
correlation near zero swings between roughly -0.4 and +0.4 across the sample
without any change in the underlying relationship. Read alone, that series looks
like a relationship forming and dissolving several times a year.

The difference between the two windows is far better behaved, because the noise
in the short window partly cancels against the noise in the long one. What
remains is the part that is actually news: the relationship has moved away from
its own history, or it has not.

## The ten-minute version

- Choose the two or three pairs that carry the diversification argument.
- Compute both windows on the same returns, with the same handling of missing
  days.
- Track the difference, and note the sample dates where it was largest.
- Re-read the original argument against those dates before trusting it.

The exercise is descriptive and it is cheap. Its value is not that it predicts
anything - it does not - but that it replaces an assumption with an observation
you can check again next quarter.
