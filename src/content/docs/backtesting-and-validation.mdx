A backtest is a conversation with the past. It can tell you that a claim is not obviously wrong,
given a sample and a set of assumptions. It cannot tell you that the claim is true. This page is
about the difference, and about the checks a result has to survive before we report it.

Every figure below is illustrative sample data.

## The shape of overfitting

Fit enough configurations to one history and one of them will look excellent. That is arithmetic,
not skill.

If every configuration has the same expected Sharpe ratio of zero and the same estimation noise,
the best of $N$ tries is biased upward. For roughly Gaussian estimates the expected maximum grows
with the number of trials:

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[\max_i \hat{S}_i\right] \approx \bar{S}
+ \sigma_S \sqrt{2 \ln N}.
$$

The lesson is not that search is forbidden. It is that the number of trials is part of the result
and has to be reported with it.

## In-sample, out-of-sample, and the wall between them

The only honest test of a rule is on data the rule has never seen.

- **In-sample** is where parameters are estimated. Performance here is an upper bound on nothing.
- **Out-of-sample** is held back during estimation and used once, at the end. Used more than once,
  it slowly becomes in-sample.
- **A fixed hold-out** is the simplest discipline and the easiest to erode. Every glance at it is a
  withdrawal.

When a sample is short, holding back a fifth of it can leave too little to fit. That is a reason to
prefer a sequence of out-of-sample windows over a single split.

## Walk-forward validation

Walk-forward validation re-fits the model on a moving window and scores it only on the step ahead.
It mimics the way a model would actually be used, and it produces a path of out-of-sample results
rather than one number.

The cost is compute and the risk of a subtler bias: if any choice was informed by the whole sample,
the walk-forward is contaminated even though no individual fold overlaps.

## Checks we run before reporting

No single check is decisive. Together they catch most of the ways a backtest can flatter itself.

1. **Shuffle or block-shuffle test.** Re-run the pipeline on data with the predictive structure
   destroyed. A strategy that still performs is reading noise.
2. **Parameter sensitivity.** Vary the parameters across a neighbourhood. A cliff at the optimum is
   a warning; a plateau is reassurance.
3. **Cost sensitivity.** Raise assumed spreads and slippage until the edge disappears. Report the
   level at which it does.
4. **Regime split.** Score the rule in calm and in stressed sub-samples separately, and report both.
5. **Multiple-testing adjustment.** When many configurations were tried, discount the best result
   by the number of trials.

## A worked illustration

An illustrative search over **60** parameter pairs reports a best in-sample Sharpe of **1.8**. With
an illustrative spread of estimation noise of $\sigma_S = 0.4$, the deflation for 60 trials is
about $0.4 \times \sqrt{2 \ln 60} \approx 1.2$, which would leave an expected best near **0.6**
even if no configuration had any edge at all.

That is not a verdict on the strategy. It is a statement of how much of the headline number the
search itself can manufacture, and it is the reason the out-of-sample figure - not the in-sample
one - is what we quote.
