A model can only be as good as the series it is fitted to, and a series is never simply "the data".
It is a set of choices about where the numbers came from, how they were aligned, and what was done
about the gaps and the obvious errors. This page describes the principles we apply. It is
deliberately generic: the specifics change with every study.

The counts and dates used below are illustrative sample figures. They are here to make a step
concrete, not to describe any real series we hold.

## Provenance comes first

Before a series is used, we record where it came from: the venue or provider, the field, the
timestamp convention, and the moment it was retrieved. A file without that record is not data, it
is a number that happens to be in a spreadsheet.

Provenance is what makes a result reproducible. If a series is revised upstream after we publish,
the retrieval date is how we tell the original sample from the revised one.

## Alignment and time

Two series can only be compared once they share a clock. The three decisions that cause the most
trouble are the timestamp convention, the calendar and the frequency.

- **Timestamp convention.** A daily bar can be stamped at its open, its close or its settlement.
  Mixing conventions across two series manufactures a lead or a lag that is an artefact.
- **Calendar.** Trading calendars, holidays and weekend handling differ between venues. Aligning
  by date alone silently drops or duplicates observations around a boundary.
- **Frequency.** Moving to a lower frequency is a choice of aggregation. A closing price, an
  average and a last-observation-carried-forward are three different series, not three views of
  one.

We align on a single explicit calendar, keep the timestamp convention in the schema, and record
any aggregation rule next to the series it produced.

## Missing values

Missing data is not a single problem, and the right response depends on why the value is missing.

- A **missing observation** - a day the market did not trade - is usually left out, with the
  calendar carrying the reason.
- A **missing value** inside a continuously observed series is a gap. We prefer a documented,
  reversible fill to an invisible one, and we always record how many points were filled.
- A **stale value** - a price repeated because the instrument did not trade - looks like data and
  behaves like a gap. It has to be detected, not filled.

Rule of thumb: if filling a gap changes the sign of a conclusion, the conclusion was never strong.

## Outliers and suspicious prints

An extreme value is either information or an error, and the two look identical in a series. We
treat an outlier as an error only when there is corroborating evidence - a crossing of the bid and
the ask, a price that is inconsistent with the surrounding book, a timestamp outside the session.

Deleting an outlier because it is inconvenient is one of the quiet ways a backtest is made to look
better than it is. The honest option is to winsorise, to model the tails explicitly, or to keep it
and report the sensitivity.

## The cleaning trail

Every transformation is recorded so the raw file can be recovered and the cleaned series rebuilt.
A pipeline that cannot be re-run on the raw input is a pipeline that cannot be audited.

In practice that means the raw snapshot is immutable, each cleaning step is a separate function
with its own test, and the modelled series is a named artefact of the steps rather than a file
someone edited by hand.

## A worked illustration

Imagine a daily series with **1,240** observations, of which **3** are missing mid-week and **2**
sit outside the plausible range for the instrument. An illustrative cleaning recipe is:

1. Drop the **3** interior gaps and record their dates.
2. Flag the **2** suspicious points, winsorise them to the 0.5th and 99.5th percentiles, and keep
   a note of the original values.
3. Recompute the summary statistics before and after, and report both.

If step 3 shows the mean moving by more than a rounding error, the cleaning is part of the result
and belongs in the write-up, not in a footnote.
