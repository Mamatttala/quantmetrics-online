A metric is a decision about what to look at, and every one of them is blind to something. This
page defines the measures we report most often, in the form we use them, and names what each one
leaves out.

All figures on this page are illustrative sample data.

## Value at risk

Value at risk answers a narrow question: over a given horizon, at a given confidence level, how
much can be lost? Formally, the loss $L$ exceeds the quantile only with probability $1 - \alpha$

$$
P\left(L > \operatorname{VaR}_\alpha\right) = 1 - \alpha.
$$

Under a normal assumption with mean $\mu$ and volatility $\sigma$, the parametric form is

$$
\operatorname{VaR}_\alpha = -\left(\mu + z_\alpha \sigma\right),
$$

where $z_\alpha$ is the standard normal quantile at $\alpha$. For $\alpha = 0.99$, $z_\alpha$ is
about $-2.33$, which is where the familiar multiple comes from.

Three properties matter more than the formula. VaR says nothing about the size of the loss beyond
the threshold. It is not sub-additive, so the whole can appear riskier than the parts. And it is
only as good as the distributional assumption behind it, which is exactly the assumption that fails
in a crisis.

## Expected shortfall

Expected shortfall is the average loss in the tail VaR has already crossed

$$
\operatorname{ES}_\alpha = \mathbb{E}\left[L \mid L > \operatorname{VaR}_\alpha\right].
$$

It answers the question VaR dodges - how bad is bad - and it is sub-additive, which is why
regulatory practice has moved toward it. The cost is that it is harder to estimate, because the
tail contains the fewest observations.

## Drawdown

Drawdown measures distance from a previous peak, and it is the number that describes what an
investor actually experiences. With a portfolio value path $PV_t$,

$$
\operatorname{DD}_t = \frac{PV_t - \max_{s \le t} PV_s}{\max_{s \le t} PV_s}.
$$

Drawdown is expressed as a negative percentage. Two derived quantities are reported alongside it.

- **Maximum drawdown** is the worst value of $\operatorname{DD}_t$ over the sample.
- **Duration** is the time from peak to recovery, which the depth alone does not capture.

An illustrative path that falls **22%** and takes **14 months** to recover has a maximum drawdown of
**-22%** and a duration of **14 months**; the annualised return over the same window says nothing
about how that felt.

## Sharpe ratio

The Sharpe ratio divides excess return by volatility, and it is the most quoted and most abused
number in performance reporting.

$$
S = \frac{\bar{r} - r_f}{\sigma_r}.
$$

It assumes returns are roughly independent and identically distributed, and it penalises upside
volatility exactly as much as downside. It is also trivially inflated by short samples, by
autocorrelated returns, and by choosing a start date after a bad run. When we quote it, we quote the
sample length beside it.

## Reporting discipline

Four habits keep these numbers honest.

1. Always state the horizon and the confidence level; a VaR without them is meaningless.
2. Report the sample length with any ratio.
3. Pair a return measure with a drawdown measure, because one alone invites a bad decision.
4. Label every illustrative figure, so a worked example is never mistaken for a tracked result.
